\section{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

M dwarfs are the most numerous stars in the Galaxy and the most common planet hosts, which places them at the centre of the search for habitable worlds \citep{Bonfils_2013, Dressing_2015, Gaidos_2016, Henry_2018}. Their low luminosities bring the habitable zone close in \citep{Kopparapu_2013}, where planets are easier to detect \citep{Charbonneau_2007} but also more exposed to stellar activity \citep{Shields_2016}. M dwarfs remain magnetically active for very long, and the resulting high-energy radiation and particle flux drive star--planet interactions \citep{Vidotto_2018} that shape the atmospheres and habitability of the planets they host \citep{Hebrard_2016, Dumusque_2021, Bellotti_2022}. A growing body of work has measured and characterised these small-scale magnetic fields \citep{Saar_1985, Johns_1996, shulyak+2014, Kochukhov_2021, reiners+2022, cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b, Donati_2025}.

These fields are continuously regenerated by a hydromagnetic dynamo, in which rotation organises the turbulent motions of the convective interior and converts part of their kinetic energy into magnetic energy \citep{Parker_1955, Charbonneau_2013}. The strength of this activity is governed by the Rossby number $\mathrm{Ro}=P/\tau$, the rotation period $P$ relative to the convective turnover time $\tau$: more rapidly rotating stars (smaller $\mathrm{Ro}$) are more magnetically active, until the activity saturates below $\mathrm{Ro}\approx0.1$ \citep{Wright_2011}. M dwarfs are especially relevant here because their deep or fully convective interiors and slow loss of angular momentum keep them magnetically active for billions of years \citep{Reiners+2012}. In the most active M dwarfs the surface field strength itself follows the rotation rate \citep{shulyak+2014, Kochukhov_2021, reiners+2022}, and much of this field emerges not as a single global structure but as tangled, small-scale flux.

Most of the magnetic flux on an M dwarf resides in small-scale, mixed-polarity structures that circular polarimetry cannot recover, because opposite polarities cancel in the net Stokes V signal \citep{donati_landstreet+2009, Kochukhov_2021}. These photospheric fields nonetheless leave a measurable imprint on the unpolarised Stokes I spectrum: when a magnetic field is present, energy levels split into sublevels due to the Zeeman effect, resulting in broadening of magnetically sensitive lines \citep{Landi_2004, reiners_basri_2007, Reiners+2012, shulyak+2014}. Because these fields reshape the spectrum, neglecting them biases the atmospheric parameters derived from it, while poorly constrained atmospheric parameters in turn bias the inferred field. Measuring the surface magnetic fields of M dwarfs is therefore a prerequisite for both stellar and exoplanetary characterisation.

Both quantities are accessible from the same data. Previous studies \citep{Rajpurohit_2018, Passegger_2019, Marfil_2021, Sarmento_2021} have used high-resolution near-infrared spectrometers, such as CRIRES+ \citep{Crires}, CARMENES \citep{Carmenes}, or SPIRou \citep{spirou}, to constrain the atmospheric properties of tens to hundreds of stars, and the same observations make it possible to measure the field itself by modelling the shape of the lines it broadens \citep{kochukhov+2020, Petit_2021}. Recent works, like \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b, reiners+2022, Hahlin_2023, Petit_2021}, have used the Zeeman broadening as a quantitative tool by fitting M-dwarf spectra against grids of magnetic synthetic spectra. These grids represent the surface as a combination of components at different strengths, each weighted by the fraction of the disc it covers, its magnetic filling factor \citep{Kochukhov_2021}, as the disc-integrated profile reflects the full distribution of field strengths across the visible surface rather than a single mean value. Because the Zeeman effect scales with the effective Land\'e factor, lines of different magnetic sensitivity respond differently to the same field, which helps disentangle magnetic broadening from rotation and macroturbulence. The detection of Zeeman signatures nonetheless remains difficult for weakly magnetised stars, whose lines may be dominated by rotational or macroturbulent broadening. 

Besides the new high-resolution NIR data, a large archive of optical spectra is available from the NARVAL \citep{narval} and ESPaDOnS \citep{espadons} instruments, which have observed M dwarfs repeatedly. This archive lets us test whether magnetic fields can also be retrieved from optical spectra, and whether the stellar parameters we derive agree with those from the NIR. Agreement between the two would lend confidence to both sets of retrievals, while any disagreement could point to aspects of the modelling that need improvement.

In this work, we use the spectral-analysis pipeline \texttt{asap}, developed by \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b}, to characterise the small-scale magnetic fields of weakly and moderately active M dwarfs from these archived high-resolution optical spectra collected with NARVAL and ESPaDOnS. The pipeline relies on synthetic spectra from \texttt{ZeeTurbo}, a spectral-synthesis code that solves the polarised radiative transfer equation across the stellar disc, to constrain the atmospheric parameters and magnetic fields. To compare optical and NIR results directly, we select stars that also appear in the \citet{cristofari_2023b} sample and for which archived optical data are available.

A central aim is also to keep the analysis as autonomous as possible. Rather than tuning the pipeline star by star, we apply the same line lists and configuration to every target and let the analysis adapt to each star on its own. The sample is heterogeneous, spanning a range of spectral types, effective temperatures, and magnetic field strengths, so a single fixed setup must still return reliable parameters across the full set. Keeping the procedure identical for every star also removes per-star tuning as a source of bias, which is essential if the optical and NIR results are to be compared on equal terms.
