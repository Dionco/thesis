\section{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

M dwarfs are the most numerous stars in the Galaxy and the most common planet hosts, making them central to the search for habitable planets \citep{Bonfils_2013, Dressing_2015, Gaidos_2016, Henry_2018}. Their low luminosities bring the habitable zones close in \citep{Kopparapu_2013}, where planets are easier to detect \citep{Charbonneau_2007} but also more exposed to stellar activity \citep{Shields_2016}. M dwarfs remain magnetically active for billions of years, and the resulting high-energy radiation and particle flux drive star--planet interactions \citep{Vidotto_2018} that shape the atmospheres and habitability of the planets they host \citep{Hebrard_2016, Dumusque_2021, Bellotti_2022}. A growing body of work has measured and characterised these small-scale magnetic fields: the local, tangled, mixed-polarity structures, unresolved on the stellar surface, that carry most of the magnetic flux and are probed through Zeeman broadening in unpolarised spectra \citep{Saar_1985, Johns_1996, shulyak+2014, Kochukhov_2021, reiners+2022, cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b, Donati_2025}.

The strength of this activity is governed by the Rossby number $\mathrm{Ro}=P/\tau$, the rotation period $P$ relative to the convective turnover time $\tau$. More rapidly rotating stars (smaller $\mathrm{Ro}$) are more magnetically active, until the activity saturates below $\mathrm{Ro}\approx0.1$ \citep{Wright_2011}. M dwarfs are especially relevant here because their deep or fully convective interiors and slow loss of angular momentum keep them magnetically active for billions of years \citep{Reiners+2012}. In the most active M dwarfs the surface field strength itself follows the rotation rate \citep{shulyak+2014, Kochukhov_2021, reiners+2022}, and much of this field emerges not as a single global structure but as tangled, small-scale flux.

How this dynamo operates in M dwarfs is far from settled, however. Below $\sim0.35\,M_\odot$ they become fully convective and lose the tachocline --- the sheared boundary between a star's still radiative core and its differentially rotating convective envelope, where the rotation rate changes sharply --- that is thought to help organise the magnetic field in the Sun. The dynamo that operates in the Sun therefore cannot work in the same way in M dwarfs, yet they remain strongly magnetic and the active fraction rises sharply toward the fully convective regime \citep{Chabrier+1997, West+2008}. The surface field strength itself follows the same rotation dependence as the activity, increasing with faster rotation and then saturating among the most rapid rotators, where it reaches an energetic ceiling set by the available convective power \citep{shulyak+2017, shulyak+2019, Kochukhov_2021, reiners+2022}; indeed, \citet{reiners+2022} showed that the saturation of activity is a direct consequence of the saturation of the field itself.

The strength of this activity is governed by the Rossby number $\mathrm{Ro}=P/\tau$, the rotation period $P$ relative to the convective turnover time $\tau$. More rapidly rotating stars (smaller $\mathrm{Ro}$) are more magnetically active, until the activity saturates below $\mathrm{Ro}\approx0.1$ \citep[Fig.~\ref{fig:activity_rotation};][]{Wright_2011}. This saturation reflects the field itself reaching an energetic ceiling set by the available convective power, rather than the rotation rate \citep{reiners+2022, shulyak+2017, shulyak_2019, Kochukhov_2021}. How this dynamo operates in M dwarfs is far from settled, however. The interior structure changes character near $\sim0.35\,M_\odot$ \citep{Chabrier_1997}: above this limit M dwarfs resemble solar-type stars, with a convective envelope surrounding a radiative core, but below it they become fully convective. In doing so they lose the tachocline --- the thin, sheared boundary between the still radiative core and the differentially rotating convective envelope, where the rotation rate changes sharply --- that is thought to help organise the magnetic field in the Sun, although the details of the solar dynamo itself remain unsettled \citep{Charbonneau_2014, Brandenburg_2005}. The dynamo that operates in the Sun therefore cannot work in the same way in fully convective M dwarfs, yet they remain strongly magnetic and active, showing flares, rotational photometric variability, and enhanced chromospheric and coronal emission at X-ray, ultraviolet, and radio wavelengths \citep{Hawley_2014, Newton_2016, Newton_2017, Astudillo_2017, Wright_2018, Villadsen_2019}. This straddling of the fully convective boundary offers a unique opportunity to study cool-star dynamo action in a different environment compared to the Sun, making empirical measurements of M-dwarf surface magnetism critical for guiding stellar dynamo theory.

% Most of the magnetic flux on an M dwarf resides in small-scale, mixed-polarity structures that circular polarimetry cannot recover, because opposite polarities cancel in the net Stokes V signal \citep{donati_landstreet+2009, Kochukhov_2021}. These fields nonetheless leave a measurable imprint on the unpolarised Stokes I spectrum: when a magnetic field is present, energy levels split into sublevels due to the Zeeman effect, resulting in broadening of magnetically sensitive lines \citep{Landi_2004, reiners_basri_2007, Reiners+2012, shulyak+2014}. Because these fields reshape the spectrum, neglecting them biases the atmospheric parameters derived from it, while poorly constrained atmospheric parameters in turn bias the inferred field. Measuring the surface magnetic fields of M dwarfs is therefore a prerequisite for both stellar and exoplanetary characterisation.

Measuring these fields is not straightforward, however. Because circularly polarised light (Stokes $V$) is signed, the small-scale fields are largely invisible to it: their opposite polarities --- tangled by convective turbulence and unresolved across the stellar disc --- cancel on integration, leaving polarimetry sensitive only to the large-scale field: the global, star-sized component that stays coherent across much of the surface \citep{donati_landstreet+2009, Kochukhov_2021}. The small-scale flux is recovered instead from the unpolarised intensity spectrum (Stokes $I$). A magnetic field lifts the degeneracy of an atom's or molecule's magnetic
sublevels, splitting each transition into a central $\pi$ component at the
unshifted wavelength $\lambda_0$ and two $\sigma^{\pm}$ components shifted to
either side by
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\lambda_B = \frac{\lambda_0^{2}\, e\, g_{\mathrm{eff}}\, B}
                         {4\pi m_{\mathrm{e}} c},
  \label{eq:zeeman}
\end{equation}
where $g_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the line's effective Land\'e factor, ranging from
$0$ for insensitive lines to a few units for the most sensitive
\citep{Morin_2012}. This separation is typically smaller than the thermal,
turbulent, rotational, and instrumental broadening, so the components are 
unresolved, blending into a single line that is broader
than the field-free profile (Fig.~\ref{fig:zeeman_broadening}). Because this broadening is
independent of polarity, it recovers the unsigned flux that Stokes $V$ misses. That sensitivity is also its difficulty and importance: the field reshapes the line profiles, so neglecting it biases the atmospheric parameters derived from the spectrum, which in turn bias the inferred field. Restricting the analysis to field-insensitive lines does not break this coupling. Transitions with $g_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 0$ are scarce and because the field also reshapes the atmospheric structure, even these lines are not truly field-free \citep{kochukhov+2020, Olander_2021}. A simultaneous treatment of field and atmosphere is therefore a prerequisite for accurate stellar characterisation and, through the star--planet connection, for the planetary parameters and environments that follow from it.

% Information on both the atmosphere and small-scale magnetic fields can be obtained simultaneously using the same data. Previous studies \citep{Rajpurohit_2018, Passegger_2019, Marfil_2021, Sarmento_2021, Cristofari_2022a,cristofari+2022b} have used high-resolution near-infrared spectrometers, such as CARMENES \citep{Carmenes}, or SPIRou \citep{spirou}, to constrain the atmospheric properties of tens to hundreds of stars, and the same observations make it possible to measure the field itself by modelling the shape of the lines it broadens \citep{kochukhov+2020, Petit_2021}. Recent works, such as \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b, reiners+2022, Hahlin_2023, Petit_2021}, have used the Zeeman broadening as a quantitative tool by fitting M-dwarf spectra against grids of magnetic synthetic spectra. These grids represent the surface as a combination of components at different strengths, each weighted by the fraction of the disc it covers, its magnetic filling factor \citep{Kochukhov_2021}, as the disc-integrated profile reflects the full distribution of field strengths across the visible surface rather than a single mean value. Because the Zeeman effect scales with the effective Land\'e factor, lines of different magnetic sensitivity respond differently to the same field, which helps disentangle magnetic broadening from rotation and macroturbulence. The detection of Zeeman signatures nonetheless remains difficult for weakly magnetised stars, whose lines may be dominated by rotational or macroturbulent broadening. 

High-resolution near-infrared (NIR) spectrographs such as CARMENES \citep{Carmenes} and SPIRou \citep{spirou} have been used to characterise the atmospheres of tens to hundreds of M dwarfs \citep{Rajpurohit_2018, Passegger_2019, Marfil_2021, Sarmento_2021, Cristofari_2022a, cristofari+2022b}. The spectra obtained with these spectrographs also carry the imprint of the magnetic field, whose Zeeman broadening can be modelled directly \citep{kochukhov+2020, Petit_2021}, and a growing number of studies now exploit this to retrieve the field itself \citep{reiners+2022, cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b, Hahlin_2023} by fitting observed M-dwarf spectra against precomputed grids of magnetic synthetic spectra. In this approach, the disc-integrated profile is modelled as a sum of synthetic spectra computed at several field strengths, each weighted by the fraction of the surface it covers --- its magnetic filling factor \citep{Kochukhov_2021}. The set of filling factors then describes the distribution of field strengths across the surface. Because lines of larger $g_{\mathrm{eff}}$ are broadened more by the same field (Eq.~\ref{eq:zeeman}), comparing transitions of different magnetic sensitivity helps disentangle the magnetic broadening from field-independent effects. The two main such effects are rotation and macroturbulence. In a rotating star, opposite limbs move towards and away from the observer, Doppler-shifting the line blue- and redward across the disc, so the disc-integrated profile is broadened by the projected equatorial velocity $v\sin i$. Macroturbulence comes from large-scale motions of gas in the photosphere, usually thought to be driven by convection, which shift the line in the same way. These motions are commonly split into radial and tangential flows, following the radial-tangential prescription of \citet{gray+1975}. In weakly magnetised stars, where the Zeeman imprint is small, these effects can dominate the line width and leave the magnetic signal hard to detect.

This archive lets us test whether magnetic fields can also be retrieved from optical spectra, and whether the stellar parameters we derive agree with those from the NIR. As the two regimes need not return the same field and the ways in which they can differ are themselves informative \citep{Petit_2021}. Optical and near-infrared lines form at different depths and may sample different layers of the atmosphere. The observations are also not simultaneous, so any evolution of the field between epochs enters the comparison. Because the Zeeman splitting grows as $\lambda^2$ \citep{donati_landstreet+2009, Reiners+2012}, the optical intensification signal is intrinsically weaker than in the NIR, and for the least magnetic stars there is a real risk of leaning on a signal that the optical data only marginally constrain. Agreement between the two regimes would therefore lend confidence to both sets of retrievals. A disagreement, by contrast, would not simply be a modelling error to be removed but a genuinely open question about formation depth, temporal evolution, or the sensitivity floor of optical intensification, and it is questions of this kind that make the comparison worth pursuing.

Alongside the high-resolution NIR spectra discussed above, a large archive of optical spectra is available from the NARVAL \citep{narval} and ESPaDOnS \citep{espadons} spectropolarimeters, which have observed many M dwarfs repeatedly over many years. Since magnetic fields are already known to be detectable from such optical data, the aim here is to apply the same methodology used in the NIR to stars in this archive and compare what the two regimes yield. Optical and NIR lines form at different atmospheric depths, probing different layers and hence different field regions \citep{Afram_2019, Olander_2021}. The two regimes therefore carry partly independent information and can yield differing results \citep{Petit_2021}. They are also not observed simultaneously, and because M-dwarf fields evolve on rotational and activity-cycle timescales, the optical and NIR data may sample the star in genuinely different magnetic states. Because the Zeeman splitting grows as $\lambda^2$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:zeeman}), the optical Zeeman broadening signal is intrinsically weaker than in the NIR. Given these differences, agreement between the regimes would lend confidence to both sets of field estimates, while a disagreement would point to one of several causes: a difference in formation depth, temporal evolution of the field, or the sensitivity floor of the optical magnetic signal. It is the prospect of distinguishing these that makes the comparison worth pursuing.

In this work, we use the spectral-analysis pipeline \texttt{asap}, developed by \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b}, to characterise the small-scale magnetic fields of weakly and moderately active M dwarfs from these archived high-resolution optical spectra collected with NARVAL and ESPaDOnS. The pipeline relies on synthetic spectra from \texttt{ZeeTurbo}, a spectral-synthesis code that solves the polarised radiative transfer equation across the stellar disc, to constrain the atmospheric parameters and magnetic fields. To compare optical and NIR results directly, we select stars that also appear in the \citet{cristofari_2023b} sample and for which archived optical data are available.

A central aim of this work is to keep the analysis as uniform and automated as possible. Rather than adjusting it by hand from one star to the next, we apply the same analysis to every target and let it adapt to each star automatically. This serves two purposes. First, the sample of analysed stars is diverse, spanning a range of spectral types, effective temperatures, and magnetic field strengths, and we want a single approach that recovers reliable parameters across this whole range rather than one optimised for any individual star. Second, treating every star in the same way removes case-by-case choices as a source of bias, so that any difference we find between the optical and NIR fields reflects the stars and the data rather than how each spectrum was analysed.
