\subsection{The \texttt{asap} retrieval}
\label{sec:methods_fit}
The retrieval operates iteratively. At each step the sampler proposes a parameter set, the forward model generates the corresponding synthetic spectrum, and the likelihood measures its agreement with the observation, so the proposal is accepted or rejected accordingly. This step is repeated many times, and the accepted positions trace out the posterior. We use the affine-invariant ensemble sampler \texttt{emcee}\footnote{\url{https://emcee.readthedocs.io/en/stable/}} \citep{emcee}.

\subsubsection{Forward model}
\label{sec:methods_forward}
% Synthetic spectra are pulled from the precomputed \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid and linearly interpolated over the three atmospheric parameters $(T_\mathrm{eff}, \log g, \mathrm{[M/H]})$ at each magnetic component $B_i$ spanning $0$--$10\,\mathrm{kG}$ in $1\,\mathrm{kG}$ steps. 
The forward model begins by linearly interpolating within the precomputed \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid over the three atmospheric parameters $(T_\mathrm{eff}, \log g, \mathrm{[M/H]})$ at each magnetic component $B_i$. This produces 11 spectra $S_i(\lambda)$, one per field strength, which are then linearly combined into the single spectrum $S_\mathrm{mag}$ (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_magnetic}), which is used throughout the rest of the forward model (Eq.~\ref{eq:magnetic-mixing}). Figure~\ref{fig:forward_model} traces this construction for a single Zeeman-sensitive Ti\,\textsc{i} line in Gl~388 (AD~Leo). The Zeeman broadening grows visibly with $B_i$ across these spectra.

The combined spectrum is then shifted to the stellar rest frame using the relativistic Doppler factor for a radial velocity $v_r$, whose initial value is obtained once before sampling by cross-correlating the observed spectrum against a VALD\footnote{\url{https://vald.astro.uu.se/}} absorption-line mask. The spectrum is then broadened by convolution with a kernel combining the instrumental width (a Gaussian of full width at half maximum (FWHM) $4.6\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ for NARVAL/ESPaDOnS), radial-tangential macroturbulence ($\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$), and rotational broadening (the Gray rotation profile, \citealt{gray_1992}). Rotation is neglected here, with $v\sin i$ fixed to zero, as justified by the long rotation periods measured for these stars \citep{fouque_2023, Donati_2023}, which place their equatorial velocities well below the instrumental width across the sample. Because rotation is fixed to zero, the fitted $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$ measures macroturbulence alone rather than a combination of the two effects. The fully transformed synthetic spectrum is then resampled onto a reference wavelength grid.

\subsubsection{Free parameters and priors}
\label{sec:methods_freeparams}
Every star in the sample is fitted with the same free parameters, all given uniform priors whose bounds are listed in Table~\ref{tab:mcmc_priors}. The bounds on $T_\mathrm{eff}$, $\log g$ and $[\mathrm{M/H}]$ are inherited from the \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid extents, and $f_1,\dots,f_{N_B}$ are uniform over the simplex defined in Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_magnetic}. \subsubsection{Likelihood and error estimation}
\label{sec:methods_likelihood}
Following \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b}, we adopt a pixel-wise Gaussian likelihood. With $O_i$ the observed normalised flux at pixel $i$, $M_i$ the synthetic flux returned by the forward model, and $\sigma_i$ the observational uncertainty on $O_i$, the chi-squared statistic is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:chi2}
    \chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{(O_i - M_i)^2}{\sigma_i^2} ,
\end{equation}
and the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:likelihood}
    \ln \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2}\chi^2 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln\!\left(2\pi\sigma_i^2\right) ,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of fitted pixels.

To absorb modelling-induced systematics that the pipeline uncertainties do not capture, we follow the two-pass procedure of \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b}. A first MCMC is run with the original $\sigma_i$. The residuals at the posterior median yield a reduced chi-squared $\chi^2_\nu = \chi^2/\nu$, with $\nu = n - k$ degrees of freedom for $k$ free parameters. In the subsequent run, all pixel errors are inflated as $\sigma_i \to \sigma_i\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu}$, so that the best fit has $\chi^2_\nu \approx 1$.

Each parameter's reported value is taken as the mean of the highest-likelihood $5\%$ of samples, which approximates the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate rather than the posterior median. Its associated formal error bar is given by half the width of the central $68\%$ (16th--84th percentile) interval of the second-pass posterior. These formal bars are statistical lower bounds that reflect only the width of the posterior and do not capture systematics or model error, which is why the empirical floors of \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b} are adopted as the reported uncertainties. Typical formal values are $5$--$20$~K in $T_\mathrm{eff}$ and $0.005$--$0.080$~dex in $\log g$ and $[\mathrm{M/H}]$. \citet{cristofari+2022b} found these formal errors to be significantly smaller than the dispersion of derived parameters across reasonable modelling assumptions, and proposed \emph{empirical} error bars obtained by adding in quadrature a floor of $30$~K in $T_\mathrm{eff}$, $0.05$~dex in $\log g$, and $0.10$~dex in $[\mathrm{M/H}]$.

% A careful selection of absorption lines is crucial. Not all of the observed spectrum is usable, and not all of it is equally informative. Parts are degraded by low signal-to-noise or contaminated by

A careful selection of absorption lines is crucial. Not all of the observed spectrum is usable, and not all of it is equally informative. Some regions are reproduced far less faithfully than others, because the model atmospheres used in \texttt{ZeeTurbo} remain imperfect, and these regions are best excluded. Parts are also degraded by low signal-to-noise or telluric contamination. We therefore restrict the analysis to a curated set of spectral regions by evaluating the likelihood of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood} over them. These regions alone then constrain the retrieved parameters. In what follows we use \emph{line list} for the set of spectral lines of interest and \emph{regions} for the wavelength windows around them on which the likelihood is computed.

% Each parameter alters a line's strength, width, or central wavelength in ways that partly mimic the others, so the list must be built to separate these responses rather than let them be absorbed into one another \citep{Kochukhov_2021}. The degeneracy between metallicity and magnetic intensification is a clear example: both raise the equivalent width of magnetically sensitive Fe lines. A transition's magnetic sensitivity is set by its effective Land\'e factor $g_\mathrm{eff}$, which fixes the size of the Zeeman splitting at a given field strength, so a list dominated by high-$g_\mathrm{eff}$ Fe transitions cannot distinguish a more metal-rich, weakly magnetic star from a less metal-rich, strongly magnetic one.

% These degeneracies set the selection criteria. The list should span a range of $g_\mathrm{eff}$, since comparing transitions of differing magnetic sensitivity is what separates Zeeman broadening from the rotational and turbulent broadening. Longer-wavelength lines are favoured, as the Zeeman splitting grows with wavelength \citep{donati_landstreet+2009, Reiners+2012}. The group of Ti~\textsc{i} lines near 970~nm identified by \citet{Kochukhov+2017, Shulyak+2017, Kochukhov_2021} is especially valuable for this reason. It is equally important to include at least one transition with negligible $g_\mathrm{eff}$, because such a line pins down the non-magnetic broadening, principally the macroturbulence $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$ and the stellar rotation, independently of the field, disentangling the two broadening effects.

Certain parameters leave similar imprints on the spectrum, and the list must break the resulting degeneracies. The clearest case is line broadening: separating the field from rotation and macroturbulence requires lines spanning a range of $g_\mathrm{eff}$, since only the magnetic broadening depends on it (Eq.~\ref{eq:zeeman}). Longer-wavelength lines are favoured, as the splitting grows with $\lambda^2$. The Ti~\textsc{i} group near 970~nm is especially valuable here \citep{Kochukhov+2017, Shulyak_2017, Kochukhov_2021}. Including at least one transition with negligible $g_\mathrm{eff}$ anchors the non-magnetic broadening independently of the field, disentangling the two broadening effects.

To assemble and refine this list I built \texttt{wave\_explorer}, an
interactive spectrum-exploration tool. For a given star it overlays the observed spectrum on a synthetic spectrum generated from the point estimates of the parameters of a completed pipeline run, showing directly which features the forward model reproduces and which it misses. Region boundaries can be adjusted, new windows added, and existing ones removed. The tool also reports the residuals between observation and model and assigns a quality flag from the mean pixel-wise $\chi^2$. A high value marks a systematic mismatch, such as a poorly modelled line, an unmasked telluric, or a continuum error. Sorting by this flag produces a worst-first table, letting us quickly inspect where model and data disagree most and prune or remask accordingly. On top of this, lines from the VALD database are overplotted and labelled by species, indicating which transitions are responsible for each observed feature. A screenshot of the tool is displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:wave_explorer}.

\texttt{wave\_explorer} can be run on a single star or on many at once.
Single-star mode tailors a list to one target, whereas multi-star mode builds a list that performs well across a diverse sample, as required when a common selection must serve many stars. In multi-star mode the displayed spectrum and residuals are the pixel-by-pixel averages of the observed and model flux on a common wavelength grid, which exposes the features shared across the sample. The quality flag of each region is then the median $\chi^2$ over stars, so a region is penalised only when it fits poorly for the sample as a whole rather than for a single outlier.

The curated list this process yields is what we refer to as the common line list, which comprises $29$ wavelength regions in the range $747$--$979\,\mathrm{nm}$. The species and effective Land\'e factor $g_\mathrm{eff}$ of each region are identified with the strongest VALD line falling inside the region. Table~\ref{tab:linelist_opt} reports the resulting identifications. The list is split roughly evenly between Ti\,\textsc{i} ($14$ lines) and Fe\,\textsc{i} ($13$ lines), with single Mg\,\textsc{i} and V\,\textsc{i} regions.

\subsection{Magnetic-component selection via BIC}
\label{sec:methods_bic}
% The magnetic model is described in Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_magnetic}. Its filling factors are constrained to be non-negative ($f_i \geq 0$). For weakly magnetic stars this introduces an asymmetry: the filling factors of the strong-field components cannot scatter below zero, so noise pushes their posterior means to small positive values rather than averaging out around zero. Because the mean field $\langle B\rangle = \sum_i B_i f_i$ weights each component by its field strength, these spurious filling factors are amplified by the large $B_i$ of the strong-field components, biasing $\langle B\rangle$ upward. The full magnetic grid therefore overestimates $\langle B\rangle$ for weakly magnetic stars, and we reduce the number of components carried as free parameters to recover a lower, less biased estimate.

Whenever a component's true filling factor lies near zero --- the lower bound of the prior --- its posterior is truncated there and its mean is displaced to a small positive value rather than averaging to zero. This boundary effect occurs in weakly and strongly magnetic stars alike, but its impact on the mean field depends on which components are affected, since each field strength is weighted by its filling factor. In a strongly magnetic star the dominant high-field components carry a genuine, well-constrained filling factor and $\langle B\rangle$ is large, so the residual bias from any near-bound components is a small fractional effect. In a weakly magnetic star, it is the high-field components that sit near zero. Each one still picks up a small spurious filling factor, and multiplying that by a large $B_i$ turns it into a sizeable $B_i f_i$ term. The true $\langle B\rangle$ is small, so these few spurious terms can make up a significant fraction of the sum and push it upward. Therefore, as in \citet{Lavail_2019}, we use the BIC to limit the number of components carried as free parameters, dropping high-field components the data do not require.

We select the number of magnetic components per star with the BIC \citep{Schwarz+1978, Sharma_2017},
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bic}
    \mathrm{BIC} = -2 \ln \mathcal{L} + k\,\ln n,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the likelihood (Eq.~\ref{eq:likelihood}) evaluated at the production best-fit (maximum-likelihood) parameters, $k$ is the number of free parameters, and $n$ is the number of fitted spectral pixels. The first term rewards goodness of fit, while the penalty $k\ln n$ grows with model complexity, so an additional component is justified only when it improves the likelihood enough to outweigh the penalty. The configuration with the lowest BIC is preferred. Locating this minimum directly would require a separate MCMC run for each number of components, which is very expensive across the full sample. We therefore approximate the BIC with a rapid \emph{scan}.

% We apply this post hoc, without re-running the MCMC. The production run (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_filter}) uses a magnetic grid spanning $0$--$10\,\mathrm{kG}$ in $1\,\mathrm{kG}$ steps. From its best-fit solution we compute the BIC of the full configuration and then collapse the grid from the top down. At each step the strongest component is removed and its filling factor transferred to the next-strongest component, the synthetic spectrum is recomputed with all other parameters held at their best-fit values, and the likelihood and BIC are re-evaluated, lowering $k$ by one. Transferring the filling factor to the adjacent bin rather than discarding it lowers $\langle B\rangle$ by only $f_\mathrm{drop}\,\Delta B$ per step (with $\Delta B = 1\,\mathrm{kG}$ the grid spacing), so the collapse traces a smooth, monotonically decreasing sequence in $\langle B\rangle$. We repeat until only the non-magnetic component remains and adopt the lowest-BIC configuration. The chosen configuration is then rerun in the production phase with the star's filtered line list from the diagnostic phase, from which the reported $\langle B\rangle$ is derived. Figure~\ref{fig:bic_scan_ds_leo} illustrates this scan for Gl~410, where the BIC turns over once the unsupported high-field components have been stripped. Because removing a genuine strong-field component sharply degrades the fit while removing a spurious one barely changes it, the criterion is self-regulating.

We perform this scan after the production phase (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_filter}), evaluating each candidate post-hoc without further MCMC sampling. We compute the BIC of the best-fit solution, then collapse the field grid downward, starting from the highest-field component. At each step we remove the strongest remaining component and transfer its filling factor $f_\mathrm{drop}$ to the next component down, keeping the filling factors normalised to unity ($\sum_i f_i = 1$).
