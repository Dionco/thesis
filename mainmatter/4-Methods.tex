\section{Methods: characterising spectra with an MCMC}
\label{sec:methods}

This chapter describes how we extract atmospheric and magnetic parameters from the master spectra produced in Sect.~\ref{sec:obs_preparation}. The retrieval is a Bayesian MCMC fit: we sample the posterior distribution of a parameter vector and report point estimates (posterior medians) and credible intervals derived from it. The parameter vector spans $T_\mathrm{eff}$, $\log g$, $\mathrm{[M/H]}$, $v_r$, $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$, and a set of discrete filling factors $f_i$ defined on a grid of magnetic field strengths $B_i$ over the $0$--$10\,\mathrm{kG}$ range. The synthetic spectrum compared to the data is not drawn from the ZeeTurbo grid directly; it is built from the grid at every step by interpolation, magnetic mixing, Doppler shifting and broadening.

Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_linelist} describes the atomic and molecular line list common to all stars, drawn from the VALD3 database [CITATION NEEDED: vald3]. Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_filter} introduces a per-star, $\chi^2$-based filter that prunes spectral regions in which the forward model fits the data poorly, allowing the use of a uniform initial list across a heterogeneous sample. Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_bic} introduces a post-hoc $\mathrm{BIC}$ scan that selects the number of magnetic filling-factor components per star, in contrast to the fixed magnetic grids used by \citet{cristofari+2022b, cristofari_2023b, reiners_2022}. Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_campaign} closes by describing how these elements are assembled into a three-phase campaign that produces the self-consistent parameter set used in the chapters that follow.

\subsection{The MCMC fit}
\label{sec:methods_fit}
% We fit each master spectrum with a Bayesian forward model. 
At every step a synthetic spectrum is constructed from the \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid, compared to the data through a likelihood, and the posterior distribution of the parameter vector is explored with the \texttt{emcee} ensemble sampler\footnote{\url{https://emcee.readthedocs.io/en/stable/}} \citep{emcee}. Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_forward} describes the forward model; Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_magnetic} how magnetic activity enters it; Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_freeparams} the free parameters and their priors; Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood} the likelihood and error treatment; and Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_sampling} the sampler configuration.

\subsubsection{Forward model}
\label{sec:methods_forward}
The forward model turns a parameter vector into a synthetic spectrum that can be compared to the data; it is evaluated afresh at every MCMC step. Synthetic spectra are pulled from the precomputed \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid and linearly interpolated over the 3D box $(T_\mathrm{eff}, \log g, \mathrm{[M/H]})$ at each magnetic-field node $B_i$, with $i = 0, 1, \dots, N_B$ indexing the discrete field strengths spanning $0$--$10\,\mathrm{kG}$ in $1\,\mathrm{kG}$ steps. This produces $N_B + 1$ per-node spectra $S_i(\lambda; T_\mathrm{eff}, \log g, \mathrm{[M/H]})$ on the wavelength grid of the precomputed grid.

Following \citet{shulyak+2010, shulyak+2014, kochukhov+2020, reiners+2022, cristofari_2023a}, the stellar spectrum is modelled as a linear combination of these spectra computed for various magnetic field strengths. The interpolated per-node spectra are linearly combined in flux, weighted by the filling factors $f_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:magnetic-mixing}
  S_\mathrm{mag}(\lambda) = \sum_{i=0}^{N_B} f_i \, S_i(\lambda) ,
  \qquad \sum_{i=0}^{N_B} f_i = 1 ,
  \qquad f_i \geq 0 .
\end{equation}
The physical interpretation of this mixing and the constraints on the $f_i$ are described in Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_magnetic}.

The combined spectrum is then shifted in wavelength to the stellar rest frame. The radial velocity $v_r$ is a free parameter of the fit (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_freeparams}); from its current sample the relativistic Doppler factor
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:doppler}
    \mathcal{D}(v_r) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} , \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{v_r}{c}
\end{equation}
is evaluated, and the synthetic wavelength solution is multiplied by it. An initial value for $v_r$ is obtained once, before sampling, by cross-correlating the observed spectrum against a VALD absorption-line mask; this only seeds the walkers and is not held fixed during the fit. Finally the synthetic spectrum is broadened. Instrumental broadening is a fixed convolution set by the spectrograph resolution. Macroturbulence is a free parameter (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_freeparams}), applied in radial-tangential mode ($\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$). Rotational broadening is supported by the forward model but, for the slow-rotating M dwarfs of this sample, $v\sin i$ is deliberately fixed to zero: at the instrumental resolution rotation is unresolved, and the fitted $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$ acts as an effective broadening parameter that absorbs the combined macroturbulent and residual rotational broadening. The fully transformed synthetic spectrum is then resampled onto the observed wavelength grid.

\subsubsection{Modelling magnetic activity}
\label{sec:methods_magnetic}
The linear mixing of Eq.~\ref{eq:magnetic-mixing} treats the visible stellar surface as a mosaic of magnetic patches: each patch carries one of the discrete field strengths $B_i$ and contributes a fraction $f_i$ of the disc-integrated flux. The discrete set $\{B_i\}$ is not a free modelling choice; it is inherited from the \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid described in the previous chapter, which samples $0$--$10\,\mathrm{kG}$ at $1\,\mathrm{kG}$ resolution. The mosaic is therefore a discretisation, at the grid's resolution, of the underlying continuous distribution of surface field strengths.

Two constraints follow from this picture. Each filling factor is non-negative, $f_i \geq 0$, since a patch cannot cover a negative fraction of the visible disc. The filling factors sum to unity, $\sum_i f_i = 1$, so that the patches tile the entire visible surface. The MCMC does not sample all $N_B + 1$ filling factors independently: it samples the $N_B$ magnetic factors $f_1, \dots, f_{N_B}$, and the non-magnetic factor $f_0$ is fixed by closure, $f_0 = 1 - \sum_{i \geq 1} f_i$. The sum constraint is thus satisfied by construction, and the prior only enforces non-negativity, equivalently $\sum_{i \geq 1} f_i \leq 1$.

\subsubsection{Free parameters and priors}
\label{sec:methods_freeparams}
The MCMC samples a common parameter vector for every star in the sample: the atmospheric parameters $T_\mathrm{eff}$, $\log g$ and $[\mathrm{M/H}]$, the radial velocity $v_r$, the macroturbulent velocity $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$, and the magnetic filling factors $f_1,\dots,f_{N_B}$. All priors are uniform; their bounds are listed in Table~\ref{tab:mcmc_priors}. The bounds on the atmospheric parameters are inherited from the \texttt{ZeeTurbo} grid extents. The prior on $\{f_1,\dots,f_{N_B}\}$ is uniform over the simplex defined in Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_magnetic}. $N_B$ is not itself a free parameter, but is fixed for a given run and selected post-hoc by the BIC scan (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_bic}). The remaining parameters supported by \texttt{asap}, $v\sin i$ (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_forward}), $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$, the veiling factor, and any additional Gaussian broadening, are held fixed and set to zero across the sample.

\subsubsection{Likelihood and error estimation}
\label{sec:methods_likelihood}
Following \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b}, we adopt a pixel-wise Gaussian likelihood. With $O_i$ the observed normalised flux at pixel $i$, $M_i$ the synthetic flux returned by the forward model, and $\sigma_i$ the observational uncertainty on $O_i$, the chi-squared statistic is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:chi2}
    \chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{(O_i - M_i)^2}{\sigma_i^2} ,
\end{equation}
and the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:likelihood}
    \ln \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2}\chi^2 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln\!\left(2\pi\sigma_i^2\right) ,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of fitted pixels. For fixed $\sigma_i$, maximising $\ln \mathcal{L}$ is equivalent to minimising $\chi^2$.

To absorb modelling-induced systematics that the pipeline uncertainties do not capture, we follow the two-pass procedure of \citet{cristofari_2023a, cristofari_2023b}. A first MCMC is run with the original $\sigma_i$. The residuals at the posterior median yield a reduced chi-squared $\chi^2_\nu = \chi^2/\nu$, with $\nu = n - k$ degrees of freedom for $k$ free parameters. In the subsequent run, all pixel errors are inflated as $\sigma_i \to \sigma_i\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu}$, so that the best fit has $\chi^2_\nu \approx 1$.

The 16th--84th percentiles of the second-pass posterior, taken relative to the median, define what we call the \emph{formal} error bars. Typical values are $5$--$20$~K in $T_\mathrm{eff}$ and $0.005$--$0.080$~dex in $\log g$ and $[\mathrm{M/H}]$. \citet{cristofari_2023b, cristofari_2023a} found these formal errors to be significantly smaller than the dispersion of derived parameters across reasonable modelling assumptions, and proposed \emph{empirical} error bars obtained by adding in quadrature a floor of $30$~K in $T_\mathrm{eff}$, $0.05$~dex in $\log g$, and $0.10$~dex in $[\mathrm{M/H}]$. We adopt the same floors in this work.


\subsubsection{MCMC sampling}
\label{sec:methods_sampling}
The posterior is explored with \texttt{emcee}, an ensemble Markov-chain Monte Carlo sampler that uses the affine-invariant proposal of \citet{emcee_algorithm}. A population of $K$ walkers is initialised in the parameter space of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_freeparams} and advanced jointly. At each step every walker proposes a new position based on the current configuration of the ensemble. The forward model of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_forward} is evaluated at that position to produce a synthetic spectrum $M_i$, which is compared to the observed flux $O_i$ through the likelihood of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood}; and the proposal is accepted or rejected with a probability set by the ratio of posterior densities at the new and current points. Over many steps the walker positions trace out the posterior distribution, from which the point estimates and error bars of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood} are computed.


\subsection{Line list}
\label{sec:methods_linelist}

A careful selection of absorption lines is crucial. Not all of the observed spectrum is usable, and not all of it is equally informative. Parts are degraded by low signal-to-noise or contaminated by
telluric features, and the forward model reproduces some regions more
faithfully than others. We therefore restrict the analysis to a curated set of spectral regions, since the likelihood of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood} is evaluated only over them: these regions alone determine which features drive the joint posterior over $T_\mathrm{eff}$, $\log g$, $\mathrm{[M/H]}$, $v_r$, $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$, and the filling factors $f_i$. In what follows we use \emph{line list} for the set of spectral lines of interest and \emph{regions}
for the wavelength windows around them on which the likelihood is computed.

Each parameter alters a line's strength, width, or central wavelength in ways that partly mimic the others, so the list must be built to separate these responses rather than let them be absorbed into one another \citep{Kochukhov_2021}. The degeneracy between metallicity and magnetic intensification is a clear example: both raise the equivalent width of magnetically sensitive Fe lines. A transition's magnetic sensitivity is set by its effective Land\'e factor $g_\mathrm{eff}$, which fixes the size of the Zeeman splitting at a given field strength, so a list dominated by high-$g_\mathrm{eff}$ Fe transitions cannot distinguish a more metal-rich, weakly magnetic star from a less metal-rich, strongly magnetic one.

These degeneracies set the selection criteria. The list should span a range of $g_\mathrm{eff}$, since comparing transitions of differing magnetic sensitivity is what separates Zeeman broadening from the rotational and turbulent broadening. Longer-wavelength lines are favoured, as the Zeeman splitting grows with wavelength \citep{donati_landstreet+2009, Reiners+2012}. The group of Ti~\textsc{i} lines near 970~nm identified by \citet{Kochukhov+2017, Shulyak+2017, Kochukhov_2021} is especially valuable for this reason. It is equally important to include at least one transition with negligible $g_\mathrm{eff}$, because such a line pins down the non-magnetic broadening, principally the macroturbulence $\zeta_\mathrm{RT}$ and the stellar rotation, independently of the field, disentangling the two broadening effects.

To assemble and refine this list we built \texttt{wave\_explorer}, an
interactive spectrum-exploration tool. For a given star it overlays the observed spectrum on a synthetic spectrum generated from the point estimates of the parameters a completed pipeline run, showing directly which features the forward model reproduces and which it misses. Region boundaries can be adjusted, new windows added, and existing ones removed. The tool also reports the residuals between observation and model and assigns a quality flag from the mean pixel-wise $\chi^2$. A high value marks a systematic mismatch, such as a poorly modelled line, an unmasked telluric, or a continuum error. Sorting by this flag produces a worst-first table, letting us quickly inspect where model and data disagree most and prune or remask accordingly.

\texttt{wave\_explorer} can be run on a single star or on many at once.
Single-star mode tailors a list to one target, whereas multi-star mode builds a list that performs well across a diverse sample, as required when a common selection must serve many stars. In multi-star mode the displayed spectrum and residuals are the pixel-by-pixel averages of the observed and model flux on a common wavelength grid, which exposes the features shared across the sample. The quality flag of each region is then the median $\chi^2$ over stars, so a region is penalised only when it fits poorly for the sample as a whole rather than for a single outlier. The final line list adopted in this work is given in Table~\ref{tab:linelist}.

The initial optical list used for the NARVAL/ESPaDOnS configuration of the retrieval comprises $45$ wavelength regions in the range $746$--$979\,\mathrm{nm}$. The species and effective Land\'e factor $g_\mathrm{eff}$ of each region are identified with the strongest VALD line falling inside the region. Table~\ref{tab:linelist_opt} reports the resulting identifications. The list is dominated by Ti\,\textsc{i} ($24$ lines) and Fe\,\textsc{i} ($16$ lines), with single Cr\,\textsc{i} and Mg\,\textsc{i} regions, two V\,\textsc{i} regions, and one molecular MgH region.

\subsection{Per-star line-list filter}
\label{sec:methods_filter}

The line list of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_linelist} is a single compromise, built to serve the sample as a whole. No common list is optimal for every star: the targets of Table~\ref{tab:obs_summary} span a range of M spectral types, and the quality of their spectra varies from one to the next, so a region that is clean and well modelled in one target can be unusable in another. On top of the common list we therefore apply an automatic, per-star step that identifies the regions a given star cannot support, removes them, and refits that star on what remains. How many regions this removes per star, and the effect on the production fits, is taken up in the Results. [VALUE NEEDED: typical and maximum number of regions removed per star.]

\subsubsection{Motivation}

Two effects make a region's usefulness star-dependent. The first is data quality. Telluric absorption falls at different stellar-frame wavelengths depending on a star's radial velocity and the epochs at which it was observed, so a line that is free of telluric contamination in one target may be blended with it in another; low signal-to-noise can likewise render a region uninformative. The second is temperature: the strengths of lines and of their blends are strong functions of $T_\mathrm{eff}$, so a transition that is clean in a warmer M dwarf can be overwhelmed by a growing blend in a cooler one. \citet{reiners+2022} address both by tailoring the line selection to each star --- requiring a minimum signal-to-noise across each line's wavelength range, discarding lines that fall too close to tellurics given the stellar radial velocity, and, because of the temperature dependence, progressively excluding individual lines below $T_\mathrm{eff}=3750\,\mathrm{K}$ and again below $3600\,\mathrm{K}$. Rather than encode such cuts as fixed temperature rules, we let the data decide: the regions to drop are identified per star from a short diagnostic fit, described next.

\subsubsection{Two-phase architecture}

The procedure runs in two phases. In the first, \emph{diagnostic} phase the star is fitted with the full common list but a reduced number of MCMC steps ($2000$). The goal is not a converged result --- the run is deliberately short --- but a representative synthetic spectrum: the forward model evaluated at the point estimates of the diagnostic posterior yields the model flux $M_i$, which is compared with the observation region by region through the metric of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_filter_metric}. Every region whose metric exceeds the threshold of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_filter_threshold} is removed, and a per-star filtered list is written.

The result of this check sets the second, \emph{production} phase. If no region is rejected, the common list is already final for that star and the run is simply carried to the full production length ($5000$ steps; Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_sampling}). If one or more regions are rejected, the set of fitted pixels --- and therefore the likelihood --- has changed, so the star is refitted from scratch on the filtered list at the full production length, with the diagnostic output archived for reference. Either way the production run is an ordinary pipeline run in the sense of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_sampling}, differing from a standard run only in the line list it uses; it is this run that yields the reported parameters.

\subsubsection{Per-region quality metric}
\label{sec:methods_filter_metric}

The quality of each region is captured by a single number. For a region $R$ --- a wavelength window of the line list --- let $N_R$ be the number of \emph{fitted} pixels falling inside that window, that is, the pixels that survive masking and enter the likelihood of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood}. The metric is the mean pixel-wise $\chi^2$ over those pixels,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:chi2region}
    \frac{\chi^2_R}{N_R} \;=\; \frac{1}{N_R}\sum_{i\in R}\frac{(O_i - M_i)^2}{\sigma_i^2},
\end{equation}
the region's own contribution to the global $\chi^2$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:chi2}, divided by its pixel count. This is \emph{not} the reduced chi-squared $\chi^2_\nu$ of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood}: no free-parameter count is subtracted, and the normalisation is the number of pixels in the region, $N_R$, rather than the degrees of freedom of the global fit. The same quantity is what \texttt{wave\_explorer} reports when the list is assembled by hand (Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_linelist}), so the automatic filter and the interactive tool rank regions on a common footing. A region with too few valid pixels to evaluate is kept rather than rejected, so the filter never discards a region on the basis of missing data alone.

\subsubsection{Threshold}
\label{sec:methods_filter_threshold}

A region is rejected when its metric exceeds a fixed threshold, $\chi^2_R/N_R > 50$. Because Eq.~\ref{eq:chi2region} is a mean of the squared, error-normalised residual, a value of order unity means the residuals are comparable to the quoted pixel uncertainties, whereas $50$ corresponds to a mean residual of order $7\sigma$ per pixel across the region. The cut is therefore deliberately permissive: it removes only regions in gross disagreement with the model --- an unmodelled blend, a residual telluric, a continuum error --- and leaves marginal regions in the fit, where the error-inflation step of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods_likelihood} already down-weights them. The threshold is the only parameter of the filter; we adopt the value $50$ for the whole sample, so that the selection criterion, like the rest of the pipeline, is applied uniformly to every star.

\subsubsection{Wavelength bookkeeping}

Two wavelength conventions meet at this step. The line list and its region windows are given in nanometres, whereas the wavelength solution stored in the diagnostic output is in \AA. The filter converts the latter to nanometres before testing which pixels fall in which region, so the membership sum in Eq.~\ref{eq:chi2region} is evaluated consistently. The filtered list is written as a copy of the original with the rejected regions commented out --- each annotated with its $\chi^2_R/N_R$ value rather than deleted --- so that the provenance of every star's list is preserved; a companion report records the metric and pixel count of each region, kept or rejected alike.
